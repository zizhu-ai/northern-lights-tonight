This is not a solar-storm explainer. It is how to see northern lights with a dark north sky, and how to read this site’s GO / MAYBE / NO. Open a local page first. This guide does not replace a tonight card.

## How to see northern lights from the Lower 48

From the Lower 48, aurora often sits on the **northern horizon**, not overhead. A south-facing slope, a downtown skyline, or a mountain wall to the north can hide it. Leave the city if the local page says to. We do not list unverified parking lots.

If the card says horizon-only, look north along a flat, dark edge. If it says city glow, a park inside the light dome is usually still a no. Standing in Colorado or Ohio is not the same as standing under the oval in Fairbanks.

## You need real darkness

Streetlight and twilight both wash out a weak oval. On this site, slots with the sun higher than **−12°** are marked not dark yet and do not count toward GO. A phone screen in your eyes is almost as bad as a porch light. Give your eyes time.

Civil twilight is not dark enough for a faint display. “After sunset” is not the rule. If the hours table still says not dark yet, waiting on the porch does not change the sun angle.

## Wait 30–60 minutes

Aurora is not a scheduled firework. If the window is open, stay for **half an hour to an hour**. Short spikes get missed by people who step out for five minutes and leave. Patience is the part the viral clip skipped.

If the status is NO because the oval does not reach you, waiting longer does not pull the oval south. If the status is NO because of clouds, waiting only helps if the deck is actually moving — the local main issue will say so.

## A phone may show color first

Cameras collect light longer than your eyes. A green smear on a phone is not a guarantee you will see a curtain. It is a hint to keep looking. Do not treat a photo as a probability, and do not treat a photo from another state as your forecast.

A long-exposure shot from another county is not part of this product. We do not score camera settings. We score whether tonight is worth trying with your eyes in a dark north sky.

## How to read GO / MAYBE / NO

- **GO** — conditions line up well enough to try.
- **MAYBE** — possible, with a named obstacle (clouds, horizon-only, city glow).
- **NO** — not worth a special trip tonight. The oval may be out of reach, or the sky socked in.
- **UNKNOWN** — we are not guessing. Data is missing or stale.

We do not print a percent chance. A national Kp number is not a local yes. Open your city or state page for the window and the main issue. Start with that card, then this posture: face north, get dark, wait.

## What not to do

Do not drive south expecting a better oval. Do not sit on a south-facing mountain wall. Do not use high beams as a viewing aid. Do not crowd an unnamed pullout we never listed. Most of this is subtraction: remove lights, remove the wrong horizon, remove the five-minute glance.

## Clothing, eyes, and cars

Do not quit because you are cold or blinded. Dress for the wait. Dim dash lights. Keep headlights off if you are safely parked. Do not stand in a traffic lane for a horizon glow. Give your eyes ten minutes after a phone screen.

A red headlamp is kinder than a white one. It still ruins a faint display if you point it north. Point it at your boots if you must.

## Mid-latitude versus oval posture

Under the oval, color can be overhead and obvious. At mid-latitudes it is usually a low green band on the north horizon. If you keep looking up, you will miss it. If you keep looking at the brightest thing in town, you will miss it.

Bring binoculars if you like; they are optional. A tripod is for photos, not for the yes/no. This site does not score ISO.

## Checklist before you go out

1. Open the local page, not a national map.
2. Read status and the named obstacle.
3. If GO or MAYBE, check the window against −12° darkness.
4. Leave the brightest part of town if the card says glow.
5. Face north with a clear horizon.
6. Stay 30–60 minutes. Phone down except for a quick smear check.
7. Leave if the obstacle is “no oval reach.” Distance will not invent reach.

## What a phone smear is not

A green smudge on a long-exposure photo is not a second forecast, not a GO, and not a reason to drive another hour. It is optional evidence that some emission is there. Your eyes may still see nothing. That is normal on a MAYBE night.

We do not publish ISO, shutter, or white-balance recipes. Those belong on photography blogs. This page is about whether the sky is worth standing under.

## Safety

Ice, wildlife, rural roads, and cold are your problem, not the oval’s. Tell someone where you are going. Do not block a highway shoulder. Do not trespass. We will not argue with a landowner on your behalf.

## Horizon versus overhead

If the card says overhead, you can look up. If it says horizon-only, look north along the lowest dark edge you can find. Trees, a south-facing valley, or a downtown wall will hide a horizon oval. Overhead color is harder to hide; glow and clouds can still hide it.

Binoculars help some people on a faint band. They are optional. A wide view with dark-adapted eyes is the default.

## After a MAYBE

Fix the named obstacle or stay in. Glow → leave the dome if you can. Mixed clouds → wait only if the deck is actually moving. Horizon-only → get a north edge, not a prettier south view. If you cannot fix it, treat MAYBE as a no for a special trip.

## After a GO

Still wait 30–60 minutes. Still face north. Still keep white light down. GO means the gates passed, not that a curtain is guaranteed in minute one. How to see northern lights on a GO night is still that posture, not a five-minute glance.
