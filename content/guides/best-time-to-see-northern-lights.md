There is no single best clock time for the whole United States. The best time to see northern lights depends on season, latitude, and tonight’s weather. Use this page for the year and the night in general. Use a local page for tonight’s window.

## Best time to see northern lights in a year

In Alaska and other high-latitude places, the useful season is roughly **late August through mid-April**. Midsummer nights are often too bright, even when the oval is active. June in Fairbanks is usually a darkness no. That is why Interior season is not “any month you can get a cheap ticket.”

In the Lower 48, storms can arrive in any month. Longer nights from **September through March** help. A strong storm in May still needs a dark enough sky. Equinox weeks get folklore about extra activity; we still score tonight from data, not from the calendar.

Alaska’s “when to come” belongs on the [Alaska](/forecast/alaska) page, not on a separate places URL. Fairbanks has its own tonight tool. Do not merge those two searches.

## Best time to see northern lights in a night

Aurora is a night-sky event. On this site, a slot does not count until the sun is **12 degrees below the horizon**. That is later than civil twilight, and in summer it can be much later than “after sunset.”

There is **no nationwide showtime**. “What time tonight?” is answered on each local page as a window, not as one US clock. Mid-latitude looks are often late evening. Do not wait for a fake 73% minute. On a given date the usable slot is the first dark, clear-enough hour while the oval still reaches you — which may be 9 PM in one county and 1 AM in another.

If the local card says the window is empty, standing outside at a famous clock time does not create darkness or punch a hole in the clouds.

## It depends on where you are

- **Alaska Interior / Fairbanks:** darkness and a frequent oval. Season matters as much as tonight. Solstice weeks are a darkness no.
- **Northern Minnesota, Michigan’s Upper Peninsula, Maine:** the oval is closer. Quiet nights can still be no; storm nights can be yes.
- **Colorado, Ohio, Illinois, and similar mid-latitude states:** event nights only. Most nights are no. A GO here is unusual and local.
- **Seattle and other maritime west-side cities:** winter is darker but cloudier. A clear slot after a frontal passage can matter more than Kp.

Examples: [Minnesota](/forecast/minnesota), [Maine](/forecast/maine), [Colorado](/forecast/colorado), [Alaska](/forecast/alaska), [Seattle](/forecast/seattle).

## What “best time” is not

Usable hours are not a single Instagram clock, not “midnight everywhere,” and not the minute a friend in another state posted a photo. Magnetic latitude, skyglow, and the cloud deck at your headline point decide whether that photo is relevant.

We also do not treat moon phase as a nationwide kill switch. A bright moon on a horizon-only oval can block GO. An overhead storm can still be worth a look. That call is local.

## Equinox folklore versus data

September and March get more “the lights are coming” posts. Geomagnetic seasons have a real pattern, but a usable night is still a dark, clear, in-reach slot — not the calendar caption. A cloudy equinox is a no. A clear January night under the oval can be a yes. We will not print an equinox percent.

If you can only take one week of leave, Interior Alaska in September or March is the usual travel compromise: dark enough, less brutal than December, still not a guarantee. That is a season choice. Tonight remains a separate card.

## Clock time people ask for

“Is 10 PM the magic hour?” Not as a US rule. East Coast event nights often run late. Alaska winter can start earlier because darkness arrives earlier. Summer Lower 48 storms still wait for −12°. The local hours table is the clock. This guide will not invent a nationwide 10:40.

Moonrise can matter on a faint horizon oval. It does not cancel an overhead storm by itself. The live card will name moon when it blocks GO.

The best time to see northern lights is the overlap of darkness, reach, and holes in the clouds at that place. Everything else is commentary.

## Travel planning without a fake calendar

If you can only pick one week:

- **Interior Alaska:** late August–September or February–March. Dark enough, less brutal than deep winter, still weather-limited.
- **Minnesota / UP / Maine:** longer nights help. Clouds and snow squalls still veto nights.
- **Colorado / Ohio / Illinois:** do not book a week “for aurora.” Book if you already have another reason to be there, then use the tonight card.
- **Seattle:** winter is darker and cloudier. A clear slot after a front matters.

We do not sell flights. We do not guarantee a curtain in any of those windows. Season is a filter; the local page is the night.

## Darkness rule of thumb

Civil twilight is not enough. Nautical twilight is closer. This site uses sun at or below −12°. In June at high latitude that may never happen. In December it happens early. If you remember only one number from this page, remember −12°, not 10 PM.

Moon: name it when it blocks GO. Do not cancel a trip for a full moon if the oval is overhead and the sky is clear. Do cancel if the card already said horizon-only plus bright moon.
