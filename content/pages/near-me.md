## Why northern lights near me needs a place

Northern lights near me is not a national map pin that follows your phone in the background. Aurora reach, clouds, and darkness change in tens of miles. A Kp 6 night in Minnesota is not a Kp 6 night on the Chicago lakeshore. This page exists to turn a city, state, or ZIP into a local GO / MAYBE / NO.

Type the place. If we have a Wave 1 page, we send you there. If we only have a coordinate match, we send you to a live reading that is not a second indexed URL. The search should land on one answer, not on a gallery of cities you will not drive to tonight.

## City, ZIP, or GPS

- **City or state** — best when you already know the name. “Fairbanks” and “Alaska” are different pages on purpose.
- **5-digit ZIP** — useful when the city name is ambiguous. We match US ZIPs in our list; we do not geocode the whole planet.
- **Use my location** — the browser asks once. We do not store that point in an account, cookie, or remarketing pixel. If you deny permission, type a city instead.

The form never requires an account. If GPS fails, the search box still works. If you are in the southern hemisphere, we will not invent a northern-hemisphere score.

## What happens after you search

A Wave 1 match opens the indexed local page: hours, main obstacle, nearby places, and the local FAQ. That is the page to bookmark. This URL is only the router.

A live pin is for coordinates we do not give a dedicated URL. It is a reading, not a travel guide, and it stays noindex so it cannot cannibalize the city and state pages. Do not copy a pin URL into a blog as if it were the Fairbanks page.

If nothing matches, try the state name or a nearby larger city. We do not autocomplete every hamlet in Wave 1.

## Why a national “near me” widget would lie

Oval grids are local. Cloud decks are local. Civil twilight is local. Moon and skyglow are local. A widget that returned one color for the entire Lower 48 would be entertainment, not a forecast. We would rather ask for a ZIP than pretend.

Marine cloud in Seattle, Front Range glow in Denver, and midnight sun in Fairbanks are different failure modes. The search box exists so those modes are not averaged.

## How to use the result

Once you land on a local page, read the status first, then the window, then the main issue. If the issue is city glow, leaving town may help. If the issue is clouds, a darker but still socked-in hill does not help. If the issue is no oval reach, driving twenty minutes does not change magnetic latitude enough.

This page is not a parking-lot finder. We will not list unofficial pullouts. Face north, wait 30–60 minutes if the window is open, and treat a phone smear as a hint, not a second forecast.

## Privacy on this page

Location is used to pick a place, then discarded from the product. Analytics, if enabled, records a path without query strings. You can opt out on the privacy policy page. NOAA and Open-Meteo are not called from your browser.

The tool should feel like a flashlight, not like a tracker. If that ever changes, the privacy policy will say so in plain language.

## Common mismatches

- **Wrong state** — Ohio is not Michigan. The oval and the lakeshore are different. Search again.
- **City vs state** — Fairbanks is not Alaska. Chicago is not Illinois. Both pairs exist so the language people type has a page.
- **Downtown vs headline** — Colorado’s headline is not Denver. If you meant “from my apartment,” read the glow note before you treat a MAYBE as a balcony event.
- **Photo from elsewhere** — a curtain in another time zone is not your ZIP.

The search should reduce those mismatches, not add a map for its own sake. If Wave 2 adds more states later, this search box is how you will reach them. Until then, the honest answer for an unmatched place is a live pin or a nearby Wave 1 page, never a fabricated GO.

If you came here from a national Kp graphic, type the ZIP anyway. Northern lights near me is the step that graphic skipped.

## What the matcher will not do

It will not invent a Wave 1 page for a hamlet we do not cover. It will not send you to a random overseas city. It will not keep GPS running in the background. It will not store the point in an account. If the query is empty, it asks you to type something. If the query is junk, it says no match.

## After you land

Bookmark the indexed local page, not this router, if you come back often. Read status, window, obstacle. Use nearby links if you actually might drive that far. Ignore nearby links if they are other time zones you will not visit tonight.

## Examples of good queries

- `Fairbanks` → the Interior city page, not statewide Alaska.
- `Alaska` → statewide season and region, with a tonight card whose headline is the Interior.
- `80202` → a Colorado ZIP if it is in our list; otherwise try Denver or Colorado.
- `Chicago` → the city page, not Illinois.
- `Illinois` → the state page whose headline is not downtown Chicago.

Bad queries: empty string, a country name, a southern-hemisphere city you hope we will score anyway, or a street address. The matcher wants a US city, state, or ZIP.

## What you should do after a miss

Try the state. Try a larger nearby city. Use the homepage table if you only needed Wave 1. Do not keep sending GPS if you already denied permission; type instead.

If Wave 2 adds your state later, the same box will be the entry. Until then a live pin is the honest fallback, and it stays noindex.

## Why ZIP beats a national map

A ZIP picks a point. A national map averages a continent. Oval reach at that point, the cloud deck at that point, and whether the sun is below −12° at that point are the three facts a ZIP can unlock. A color for “the United States” unlocks none of them.

Northern lights near me is that ZIP (or city) step. Type it, land, then read the card. If the card is NO, believe the named obstacle before you drive. If it is UNKNOWN, wait for data rather than inventing a color.
